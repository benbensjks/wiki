Engineering a New SsrA-Based Degradation Tag (LAA-LAA) and a Bacterial Synthetic Oscillator The ATP-dependent ClpXP-SspB protease complex is responsible for the degradation of intracellular proteins and is maintained at low levels in Escherichia coli to avoid nonspecific degradation. The rate-limiting step in the protease complex leads to proteolytic queueing, where the proteins form waiting lines, and their overall degradation rate is slowed. Synthetic biologists have leveraged proteolytic queueing to design robust synthetic circuits by tagging proteins with the SsrA tag, an 11-amino acid sequence recognized by the complex. Previous work has demonstrated the binding site of each component of the ClpXP-SspB complex to the SsrA tag. However, the precise component responsible for queueing was unknown. To identify the bottleneck in the complex, we designed different SsrA tag variants depending on the chaperone binding sequences. We further overexpressed each protein in the ClpXP-SspB complex in vivo to determine how an increased amount of each component affects the tagged protein levels. Based on the degradation of the SsrA variants, upon overexpression of each component of the ClpXP-SspB system, evidence supports that ClpX (the ATP-dependent chaperone) is responsible for queueing but not ClpP (the protease) or SspB (the adapter, ATP-independent chaperone). In the process, we identified LAA-LAA, a 6-amino acid ClpX-dependent tag that degraded in vivo faster than the original SsrA tag, AANDENYALAA. We speculated that this high degradation tag could be useful in a dynamic-synthetic circuit, so we modified the well-characterized dual-feedback oscillator by replacing its original SsrA tag with the LAA-LAA tag to form the LAA-LAA-Osc oscillator. Both population and single-cell level experiments show that the new and old oscillators have distinct frequencies. Like the original oscillator, thousands of cells containing the new oscillator could be synchronized by entrainment using an external signal. Thus, the new LAA-LAA-Osc oscillator retains the original oscillator’s best characteristics (robustness to fluctuations, a steady oscillation period, and entrainment across 1000s of cells to an external signal) but oscillates at a different frequency. Introduction Intracellular protease complexes are critical for maintaining protein homeostasis by selectively degrading misfolded proteins or nonfunctional proteins and recycling amino acids. The ClpXP-SspB protease complex, a key player in E. coli protein degradation, consists of three distinct proteins: ClpX, an ATPase chaperone, ClpP, a serine protease, and SspB, an adapter protein (Figure 1a). ClpX uses ATP to unfold proteins and translocate their polypeptide chain into the proteolytic core of ClpP, which hydrolyzes peptide bonds to release amino acid monomers.− SspB translocates target proteins to the protease ClpP via ClpX (Figure 1a). a. The ClpXP-SspB system consists of 3 distinct proteins: SspB, ClpX, and ClpP. SspB binds to both the substrate protein (represented as gray spheres) and ClpX. ClpX binds to the substrate protein and ClpP. It also binds SspB bound to substrate and ClpP. ClpP forms complexes with either ClpX or ClpA. b. Specific amino acid sequences within the SsrA tag are recognized by the chaperones ClpX, SspB, or ClpA, enabling targeted substrate binding. c. Under normal conditions, proteins tagged with SsrA amino acid sequence are degraded at normal rates. However, during overloaded conditions, degradation slows as proteases become saturated, resulting in proteins forming ″waiting lines″ and awaiting degradation, a phenomenon known as proteolytic queueing. Proteins targeted by the ClpXP-SspB system recognize a sequence of amino acid, a degradation tag (e.g., SsrA tag) on the target protein. This tag can be inherently present on the protein or added post-translationally.− The SsrA tag is introduced during a quality control process known as trans–translation, which rescues stalled ribosomes (For a detailed discussion on this topic, see Moore et al. and Karzai et al.). Stalling may occur due to ribosomal errors, nonstop mRNAs (those lacking stop codons, often caused by transcriptional errors), or other translational roadblocks. The trans–translation mechanism utilizes a specialized molecule called tmRNA, which functions as both tRNA and mRNA. tmRNA delivers an alanine to the stalled ribosome and provides a short mRNA sequence encoding the SsrA degradation tag. When the ribosome translates the end of the tmRNA, it results in the addition of the degradation tag SsrA tag to the C-terminus of the nascent polypeptide chain. This process not only rescues the ribosome but also tags the faulty polypeptide for degradation, ensuring the fidelity of the protein synthesis system. The ClpXP-SspB system is the primary protease complex that recognizes SsrA-tagged proteins in Gram-negative bacteria. The SsrA sequence is often similar but not identical in all bacteria. The native E. coli SsrA tag has an 11 amino acid (AANDENYALAA) conserved sequence, with overlapping recognition motifs for different chaperones (Figure 1b). The precise recognition of SsrA-tagged proteins and their subsequent degradation are tightly regulated to prevent the unnecessary breakdown of functional proteins. Since proteolysis is irreversible, this regulation is critical to maintaining cellular homeostasis. Intracellular levels of proteases are often maintained at very low levels inside the cell to achieve tight regulation of proteolytic pathways. When many proteins are targeted for degradation, the proteases get saturated, and proteins wait in line to be degraded. This phenomenon is referred to as proteolytic queueing (proteolytic waiting lines) (Figure 1c). A good analogy to proteolytic queueing is to think of the protease as cashiers at a convenience store and the proteins as customers. During regular hours of operation, the cashiers promptly process the customers. However, during rush hour, due to the increased number of customers and limited number of cashiers, the customers have to wait in a queue to be processed, causing a delay. Proteolytic queueing is similar in that the proteins (customers) are waiting in line to be processed by the proteases (cashiers) (Figure 1c). Though this analogy fits the general picture of proteolytic queueing, it is important to note that the waiting lines are not as organized (not as straight) inside the cell as they are often in a convenience store. Bacteria take advantage of proteolytic queues to regulate multiple native cellular pathways.− The regulation of Sigma S (also called σS, σ38and RpoS, is the starvation/stationary phase sigma factor) is a well-known example of how protease saturation and proteolytic queue regulate a native pathway.− Sigma factors are master regulators of gene expression, as they initiate gene transcription. Sigma S, a stress-related sigma factor, is actively degraded by ClpXP-SspB in the log growth phase when the environment is favorable to avoid the onset of stress-related genes. If the cell encounters an unfavorable environment (stress, lack of nutrients, etc.), the number of misfolded proteins increases and overloads the protease complex. The saturation of proteases slows down the active degradation of the sigma factor, thereby increasing its half-life, which, in turn, initiates the expression of stress-related genes. In other words, the amount of Sigma S rises in the cell because it is a part of the proteolytic queue. This is a powerful mechanism of regulation because the signal for regulation does not rely on gene expression but can lead to a change in gene expression as Sigma S levels increase inside the cell. Synthetic biologists have long exploited proteolytic queueing to develop synthetic circuits that can perform novel functions.− When heterologous circuit proteins are fused with a degradation tag, the regulation of synthetic circuits is mainly based on protease complexes and/or proteolytic queueing. Without the degradation tag, proteins would be coupled to the cell division rate (effectively diluting the target proteins by about half with each division). For example, the repressilator, in which the network proteins are tagged with the SsrA tag, is uncoupled to the cell-division cycle. The decoupling of protein activity from cellular division offers significant advantages, enabling dynamic processes to unfold independently of the cell cycle. This decoupling is achieved using degradation tags, which can be tailored by modifying their amino acid sequences to control protein turnover rates. By fine-tuning these rates, dynamic synthetic circuits can be designed to operate over varying time scales, allowing for precise short-term or long-term responses. These synthetic circuits have been applied in biosensors,− biocontainment switches, biotherapeutics, bioproductions, and for studying biological clocks (biological oscillators). The E. coli SsrA tag, an 11 amino acid tag, is a widely used degradation tag in synthetic circuits where circuit proteins are fused with the tag at the C-terminus to facilitate protein degradation primarily by the ClpXP-SspB protease complex. Several research groups have altered the degradation rates of the SsrA-tagged proteins by mutating the last three amino acids of the SsrA tag from LAA to AAV, ASV, LVA, DAS, or LDD. Some studies have used hybrid tags that contain the SsrA tag with viral proteolytic tags, such as the FENIX system. Over the years, the SsrA tag and its variants have been used to build simple circuits as well as complex circuits, such as self-sustaining oscillators. Here, we compared the role of each component in the ClpXP-SspB system responsible for the formation of the proteolytic queue. We quantified the relative degradation of fluorescent proteins tagged with SsrA variants and how these variants affected proteolytic queueing. In this process, we built a novel SsrA variant, LAA-LAA, that degrades substantially quicker than the native SsrA tag. To assess the application of the LAA-LAA tag in a dynamic system, we replaced the SsrA tag in the dual-feedback oscillator with the LAA-LAA tag. The modified oscillator (LAA-LAA-Osc) had a different oscillation frequency than the original SsrA oscillator (SsrA-Osc). Like the SsrA-Osc, the LAA-LAA-Osc is entrainable by an external signal. The new oscillator was functional at a different frequency than the original oscillator. These results demonstrate the power of deconstructing the mechanism of a natural system (basic research) and how its knowledge can be directly applied to developing new, complex synthetic systems in bacteria. Results and Discussion The Proteolytic Queue Most Likely Forms at ClpX Previously, it was demonstrated that the rate-limiting step was unfolding of substrates by ClpX. However, we wanted to know if the queue would form at ClpX or other components of the system. We used an overexpression approach instead of the typical knockout approach for this system, as previous work has shown that knockouts can lead to growth defect. Growth defects will artificially change the marker level because biosynthesis and degradation of protein levels are affected by growth rates, and the rate of division alters protein levels in cells. We did not observe any apparent growth rate defect when overexpressing the components of the ClpXP-SspB protease system (Figure S1). We designed SsrA variants that can solely bind to one of the ClpXP chaperones (SspB or ClpX) and used the original SsrA tag as control. The SsrA tag has both SspB (AANDENY amino acid sequences) and ClpX chaperone (LAA sequences) binding sites (Figure 2a). The queue must be forming at a component of the system that has a lower number in the cell compared to the substrate. However, if we increased the major queueing component, then queues would not form until higher levels of the substrate were added. So, we independently overexpressed each component (SspB, ClpX, or ClpP proteins) of the system and measured CFP-tagged proteins. To identify the bottleneck, we cloned CFP tagged with SsrA variants (SsrA, LAA-LAA, and AANDENY) under an inducible hybrid promoter, Plac/ara-1, induced by IPTG and arabinose (Ara). A previous paper has shown that at least five amino acids are required for degradation by the ClpXP-SspB system in vitro. Therefore, we used the LAA-LAA tag instead of the LAA tag for the initial work. Each component of the ClpXP-SspB system (ClpX, ClpP, and SspB) was independently cloned under another inducible promoter system, PLtetO, induced by sublethal levels of Doxycycline (Dox 75 ng/mL). We also measured the degradation of CFP–No Tag when overexpressing each component. Each tag has a different degradation rate and, therefore, forms varied levels of the proteolytic queue. If the CFP level decreases because a component is overexpressed, it indicates that this component may be directly or indirectly responsible for the proteolytic queue. For comparison, we considered the CFP levels at 1 mM IPTG and 1% Ara to be at 100% when the components of the ClpXP-SspB system are not overexpressed (Dox 0 ng/mL). The CFP–No Tag control is often used because there is no known binding site for this protease system. Therefore, we expected that the overproduction of ClpXP components SspB, ClpX, or ClpP would not affect the CFP–No Tag levels. Surprisingly, increasing ClpX levels caused a ∼ 15% (p < 0.01) decrease in the fluorescent level in the CFP–No Tag control. Hence, to account for this background, we only consider any change above 15% for all the tags to be directly related to the tag degradation. When SspB was overproduced (Figure 2b (i), no tag was above the 15% cutoff. Overproduction of ClpP led to significantly lower CFP levels for only two tags: SsrA and LAA-LAA (Figure 2b (iii). However, increasing production of ClpX led to significantly lower CFP levels for all tags (Figure 2b (ii), supporting the queue formation at this component (see Table S1 for more details). These results have eliminated SspB as the site of the queue but leave two possibilities for ClpP: (1) ClpP could be the major component of the queue (not supported by the ClpX overproduction results), or (2) the increased ClpP levels increase the likelihood that the chaperone (ClpX) and the protease (ClpP) are able to locate each other in the cell faster, leading to more degradation (supported by the ClpX overproduction results). ClpP and ClpX are at very low levels inside the cell. In the log growth phase (the phase we used for all experiments), there are only about 100 ClpP complexes (each complex contains 14 ClpP) and about 75–100 ClpX complexes (each complex typically contains 6 ClpX). These are very small numbers considering the number of proteins within the cell that are degraded by this protease complex. Both proteins are typically spread throughout the vastness of the cell in low numbers, but when the ClpP levels are increased, ClpP and ClpX are more likely to interact with each other. The same is true when ClpX is overproduced; however, only the overexpression of ClpX (Figure 2b (i) showed a significant decrease in CFP for all tags. Overall, these results support that ClpX is most likely the major component of the queue and not ClpP or SspB (Figure 2c). The LAA-LAA is a High-Performance Degradation Tag While searching for the queueing component of the ClpXP-SspB system, we noticed that tags were producing vastly different degradation levels. We wondered if we could identify a degradation tag that would degrade faster than the native SsrA tag, so we made and tested several variants. (Figure 3a). We also tested several tags that other groups developed to compare our tags. The SsrA tag has been previously modified by mutating amino acids to disrupt the correct binding of one or multiple chaperones, such as the ClpA mutated sequence (AANDENYDLAA), and the ClpX mutated sequences (AANDENYALDD, AANDENYADAS). Some SsrA variants were made by adding a spacer sequence between SspB and ClpX binding sequences to enhance protein degradation (LAA+4 and DAS+4 tags). Due to overlapping binding sequences, SspB binding to SsrA inhibits ClpA binding. Hence, SspB binding is known to enhance the substrate specificity of SsrA-tagged proteins for ClpXP-SspB degradation. a. Of all the tags we tested, only LAA-LAA and SsrA2X were degraded faster than the native SsrA (Figure 3b; Table S2 for more details). We titrated SsrA, SsrA2X, and LAA-LAA tags at varying %Ara and constant IPTG concentration, 0.1 mM (Figure 3c). For LAA-LAA tagged CFP FU was significantly lower at all Ara concentrations as compared to SsrA tagged CFP or SsrA2X tagged CFP (Table S3). We do not know why the LAA-LAA tag leads to faster degradation than the native SsrA tag. It could be because the LAA-LAA tag has binding site only for ClpX, thus avoiding competition for binding with other chaperones. It is known that the SsrA tag has a high specificity to the ClpXP complex, with ≥ 90% of the SsrA tagged protein being degraded by ClpXP-SspB protease. The addition of an extra SsrA sequence or doubling the binding sites, in the case of the SsrA2X tag, may have increased the degradation of the tag by simply increasing the recognition by the protease system. However, the higher degradation for CFP- SsrA2X can only be seen at the lower expression. We proceeded to use the LAA-LAA tag over the SsrA-2X for our queueing experiments and synthetic oscillator because it has slightly faster degradation and a much shorter tag. Though we expected no degradation for CFP tagged with LAA-tag based on the Sauer group’s in vitro work, there was a 0.5-fold decrease in the CFP-LAA tag when compared to the CFPNo tag (control). There are two possibilities for this. First, another protease might recognize the LAA tag. Second, it is not uncommon for in vitro studies to not correspond with in vivo studies, considering the complex milieu of the cell. All our constructs contained a two amino acids TS-linker upstream of tags, which linked it to CFP (making it a 5-amino acid tag). The Sauer group’s in vitro work showed that 5 amino acids were required for degradation; thus, our TS linker could be acting as the additional 2 amino acids (TS-LAA tag) required for degradation. Regardless, the LAA tag only had slight degradation. The High-Performance Degradation LAA-LAA Variant Can Form a Proteolytic Queue Like the SsrA Tag Due to proteolytic queueing, the degradation rate of two proteins competing for the same protease can couple.− We wanted to examine the queueing level formed by the LAA-LAA tag, as it is a faster degrading tag. We used a self-crosstalk approach to explore the dynamics of queue formation. This method has been previously applied to determine the interference of degradation of different tags that are targeted to different proteases. Crosstalk has been mathematically studied in networks, including those with multiple proteases. Two chemical inducers were used to induce expression: Dox controls the YFP-tagged genes, and IPTG and Ara control CFP-tagged genes (Figure 4a). Both proteins were tagged with either SsrA or LAA-LAA. For the control, we used untagged fluorescent proteins. The YFP-tagged proteins were induced with Dox (0, 25, 50, 100, 150, and 200 ng/mL), and the CFP-tagged proteins were induced with constant IPTG (0.5 mM) and Ara (1%) concentrations. There was no significant difference in the untagged YFP levels with or without CFP–No tag production (Figure 4b (i). When YFP-SsrA is produced with the addition of Dox (0, 25, 50, 100, 150, 200 ng/mL) and without CFP-SsrA production, YFP-SsrA levels were low due to active degradation. However, when CFP-SsrA is produced with 0.5 mM IPTG and 1% Ara, the YFP level increases substantially due to the formation of queues (Figure 4b (ii). A similar queueing dynamic is observed with the high degradation tag, LAA-LAA tag (Figure 4b (iii) (Table S4). A New Synthetic Oscillator (LAA-LAA-Osc) was Constructed Using a High Degradation Tag, and It Oscillates at a Frequency Different from the Original SsrA-Osc oscillator Robust synthetic circuits have been made leveraging proteolytic queueing formed by SsrA tag. The original dual feedback oscillator (called SsrA-Osc henceforth) utilizes the SsrA tag for rapid turnover of its circuit components (LacI: the negative regulator, AraC: the positive regulator, GFP: the indicator protein). This oscillator frequency can be modulated by altering the concentration of IPTG or Ara (Figure 5a). We wanted to assess if the LAA-LAA tag can be used on a dynamic synthetic circuit like the SsrA-Osc. We, therefore, designed the new oscillator by replacing all the SsrA tags from the dual feedback oscillator (SsrA-Osc) with the LAA-LAA tag and named this oscillator LAA-LAA-Osc. First, we tested these variations of dual-feedback oscillators in a batch culture using a microplate reader. Each oscillator will have its own frequency, so typically, population assays would not be effective because there would be no clear peaks or valleys in the signal output. However, we leveraged the ability of the dual-feedback oscillator to be entrained. a. There were differences in the oscillation pattern between the two oscillators (Left). The oscillatory period increases with increase in the inducer concentration (Arabinose) (Right). Entrainment is a specific type of synchronization where an external signal leads to a cohesive response (the bulk of oscillators in a population collectively agreeing to a common oscillatory frequency). The classic biological example is circadian rhythm in humans, where our internal clock (linked to a rhythm in gene expression) follows the day and night cycles. This internal clock is imperfect, which can result in irregular sleep schedules. Our bodies readjust (entrain) our internal clock by comparing its internal time to the light from the Sun. Because SsrA-Osc is entrainable, we hypothesized that our LAA-LAA-Osc would be too, and if so, we could measure the oscillating frequency at the population (batch) level. As expected, both the SsrA-Osc and LAA-LAA-Osc oscillators were entrained (Figure 5b, left). Qualitatively, the oscillating frequency appeared to be different between the oscillators. Quantification of the period from LAA-LAA-Osc shows that the period increases with the increase in the inducer concentration, like the SsrA-Osc (Figure 5b, right). Though the population assay gives us strong evidence for oscillation and entrainment of the LAA-LAA-Osc, it does not show what is happening at the single-cell level. Without single-cell data, we could not conclusively state that this is an entrainable oscillator that has a different frequency than the SsrA-Osc. So, we tested the LAA-LAA-Osc oscillator at the single-cell level using microfluidics and microscopy. We tracked cells with SsrA-Osc (n = 71) and LAA-LAA-Osc (n = 47) in a microfluidics device. Like the plate reader data, we found no significant difference in the growth rates between them (p = 0.87) (Figure S3). The single-cell data showed oscillations of the LAA-LAA-Osc (Video S1), and its frequency was different than that of SsrA-Osc (Video S2) (Figure 5c, left). SsrA-Osc has an average period (n = 33) of 47 min. (peak-to-peak analysis) (Table S5a) or 49 min. (Fourier transformation) (Table S5b). However, LAA-LAA-Osc (n = 13) has an average period of 68 min. (peak-to-peak analysis) or 72 min. (Fourier transformation) (Figure 5c, right). This is consistent with the population data showing that the LAA-LAA-Osc has a significantly longer oscillation period. We also tested whether entrainment could be observed at the single-cell level. Indeed, we see clear entrainment of the LAA-LAA-Osc (Figure 5d) (Video S3). Though population (batch) entrainment experiments are not as informative as single-cell data, our data clearly show that population data can be used to infer many aspects of entrainable oscillators. Conclusion We investigated the mechanism of proteolytic queueing in the bacterial ClpXP-SspB complex, which is involved in protein degradation. We identified that ClpX is the most likely component responsible for the queueing effect. We also developed a new high degradation SsrA tag variant, LAA-LAA, that was degraded faster than its parent SsrA tag. This new tag was incorporated into a synthetic oscillator, resulting in a different frequency than the original design but retaining the ability to entrain. These findings highlight the potential of understanding natural systems to develop new and complex synthetic systems in bacteria. It also demonstrates how very small changes, going from an 11 amino acid degradation tag of AANDENYALAA to a 6 amino acid tag of LAA-LAA, can result in major changes in a synthetic system. Often, in synthetic biology, the pursuit is more extensive genetic circuits with greater numbers of components to develop more dynamic output and control; however, here, we demonstrated that small changes could alter a dynamic system’s output while maintaining the system’s robustness. Plasmid pKmJ271 was made by replacing the SsrA-tag in pTDCL7 with the LAA-LAA tag from each component of the dual-feedback oscillator. The microfluidics experiment was done following the protocol previously described using a previously designed microfluidic device. Cells were diluted (1:100) from an overnight culture in 100 mL of MMB+ glucose media and incubated until the OD was between 0.5 and 0.6 (exponential phase). The cells were pelleted down by centrifugation and resuspended in 50 mL of MMB+ (MMB media with 0.5% glucose (w/w), 20 amino acid mix (40 mg/mL), 2 mM MgSO4.7H2O, 0.1 mM CaCl2, thiamine (10 mg/mL), and 0.2% Tween80 (v/v). All media contained 1 mM IPTG, but only inlet I2 contained 1% ara (inducer) and sulforhodamine (50 mg/mL) (Marker Gene Technologies, Inc.). Cell Segmentation and Tracking Individual cells of pJ271 and pTDCL7 were segmented using DeLTA 2.0. We manually tracked individual cells and their offspring for approximately 5 h using ImageJ. Specifically, cell centroid and stack position were used to locate the tracked cells. The average GFP intensity of individual cells was calculated using segmentation masks and in-house Python scripts with some modifications of what we previously used. Cells containing the plasmids pTDCL7 or pJ271 were randomly selected to track across different frames of the phase contrast images and to calculate the average doubling time. To calculate the active growth rate, we excluded cells that have a doubling time of over 120 min. GFP intensity of individual cells was manually tracked and recorded over time. Peaks were identified using the find peaks function from the signal module of the SciPy package (Python v3.9.16). The oscillation periods were calculated by peak-to-peak, or Fourier transform, using a customized Python script.
